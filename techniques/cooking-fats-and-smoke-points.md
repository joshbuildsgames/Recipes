# Cooking Fats and Smoke Points

## Reference table

| Fat | Approximate smoke point | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Refined avocado oil | 500–520°F | Highest common. Neutral. Maximum margin for error. |
| Refined safflower | 510°F | Neutral, inexpensive |
| Rice bran | 490°F | Neutral, mild |
| Ghee | 480°F | Butter flavor without the milk solids that burn |
| Refined peanut | 450–470°F | Slight flavor, good for frying |
| Refined / "light" olive | 465°F | Lets you use olive oil at real searing heat |
| Grapeseed | 420°F | Neutral, cheap, goes rancid faster — buy small bottles |
| Beef tallow | 400°F (up to ~420°F if very pure) | Best flavor for steak |
| Extra-virgin olive | 350–400°F | Finishing oil. Not a searing oil. |
| Unrefined avocado | ~375°F | Lower than tallow — check the label |
| Butter | ~250–300°F | Milk solids brown then burn |

## Refining matters more than the source

The free fatty acids and plant particles left in unrefined oil are what start smoking.
Processing raises the ceiling and strips flavor at the same time.

- Avocado: 375°F unrefined vs 500°F+ refined
- Olive: 350–400°F extra-virgin vs 465°F refined

**Always check the label for "refined."** Two bottles of the same oil can differ by 125°F.

## Smoke point is not the whole story

You rarely need 500°F. Steak sears fine at 400–425°F; going higher scorches the exterior
before the interior gets anywhere. Flavor and stability matter more in practice, which is
why tallow at 400°F is still the better steak fat despite the lower number.

Avocado oil's real advantage is margin for error, not performance.

## Spray vs pour

Spray lays down a thin, uneven film. On stainless with anything dredged in flour, the dry
patches are where food grabs. **Pour a measured amount — a teaspoon or two — and swirl to coat.**

Sprays also contain lecithin and propellants that burn well below the oil itself, producing
smoke that looks alarming but is not the oil degrading.

Spray is more tolerable for steak, which renders some of its own fat immediately and has no
flour to catch. Even then, expect less thermal contact and give the meat an extra 30 seconds
before trying to move it.

## Combining fats

The classic olive-oil-plus-butter pairing is doing real work: the oil raises the effective
smoke point and buffers the butter. But across multiple batches the milk solids will still
go from brown to black.

Better options:
- Sear in a high-smoke-point fat, and save **all** the butter for finishing the sauce. The
  sauce is where you actually taste it.
- Or use ghee for the sear and regular butter in the sauce.

## Making your own tallow

Ask a butcher for beef fat — often a couple dollars a pound, sometimes free. **Suet** (the
hard fat around the kidneys) renders whiter and more neutral than general trim fat.

1. Freeze the fat 30 minutes; cold fat cuts much more easily.
2. Trim off any meat or blood spots — these are what turn tallow rancid and gamey.
3. Cut into small cubes or run through a food processor.
4. Heavy pot, splash of water (~¼ cup) to prevent scorching before fat starts flowing.
5. **Low heat, 2–4 hours.** High heat browns the fat and gives a smoky, off-tasting result.
   A slow cooker on low or a 225°F oven is more forgiving than the stovetop.
6. Done when the solids have shrunk to crisp bits and sunk, and the liquid runs clear gold.
7. Strain through fine mesh lined with cheesecloth into clean jars.

Storage: several months refrigerated, over a year frozen, a few weeks at room temperature if
well strained. Residual solids lower the smoke point and shorten shelf life, so strain carefully.

The crispy bits in the strainer are cracklings. Salt them and eat them.

## Recommended to keep on hand

- **Refined avocado oil, bottled** — neutral, maximum headroom
- **Ghee** — butter flavor that survives real heat
- **Beef tallow** — specifically for steak, where the added fat flavor does noticeable work
- **Light/refined olive oil** — alongside the extra-virgin, for when olive flavor is wanted at heat
