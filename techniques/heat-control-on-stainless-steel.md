# Heat Control on Stainless Steel

The single most important skill for cooking on stainless. Stainless is not forgiving of
a pan that is too cool, and not forgiving of butter in a pan that is too hot. Nearly every
sticking or burning problem traces back to preheating to the wrong target.

## The two-tier water test

Preheat the **empty** pan, then flick in a few drops of water.

| Behavior | Approximate surface temp | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| Sizzles hard, gone in 1–2 seconds | 300–375°F | Chicken cutlets, fish, anything dredged in flour, anything cooked in butter |
| Beads up and skates around the pan (Leidenfrost) | 375–425°F | Steak, hard sears in high-smoke-point oil |
| Silent, or slow lazy bubbling | Too cool | Wait |

The skating-bead test is the one most people learn first, and it is **too hot** for
butter-and-olive-oil cooking. Olive oil smokes on contact at that temperature and butter
burns almost immediately.

## Why food sticks — and why it releases

Protein bonds to hot metal on contact. Those bonds break as the contact surface dehydrates
and browns. So release is a function of **crust formation**, not of pan temperature per se.

- Inside the 300–375°F window, food will release if you wait long enough. The only tradeoff
  is time and crust color.
- Below roughly 250°F, it never releases. The food weeps moisture, poaches in its own juice,
  and stays bonded. **This is the real sticking nightmare, and it comes from a pan that is
  too cool, not too hot.**
- A flour dredge accelerates release: dry starch browns fast and forms a rigid crust sooner.
  Undredged protein still releases, it just takes longer and browns paler.

Practical rule: lay it down, leave it alone, and if it will not budge, give it another
30 seconds rather than forcing it.

## Cooling a pan that has overshot

Turning the burner knob down does almost nothing for a minute or more. Tri-ply and clad
pans hold a lot of energy.

**Lift the pan off the burner for 30–60 seconds.** That actually drops the surface temperature.

If the oil has smoked and darkened, wipe the pan out with a paper towel and start with fresh
fat. Burnt oil tastes acrid and will carry into any pan sauce built on that fond.

## Reading the oil

Oil viscosity is a better early warning than smoke. As it heats, oil thins and moves in
visible ripples across the pan surface. **That shimmer arrives roughly 50–75°F before smoke.**

By the time oil smokes it has already begun degrading into acrolein and free fatty acids.
Smoke is not a warning that you are approaching too hot; it is confirmation you have arrived.
Smoke also does not tell you how far past you are — 410°F and 460°F look about the same.

## Egg temperatures

Eggs set at 145–180°F and turn rubbery above that. They run cooler than everything else.

| Result | Target | Water test |
|---|---|---|
| Soft scrambled | 250–275°F | Lazy sizzle, several seconds to evaporate |
| Fried, sunny side / over easy | ~300°F | Bottom of the chicken range |
| Crispy fried, lacy brown edges | 325–350°F | Chicken range, and use more fat |

Butter is a decent gauge: it should foam gently, not brown quickly. Use more fat than feels
reasonable — a tablespoon or so — and let the eggs sit a minute before moving them.

Stainless is genuinely harder on eggs than on anything else. If a nonstick pan is available,
eggs are the one dish worth reaching for it.

## Calibration

Worth doing once: deliberately heat cheap oil until it smokes, and note the burner setting
and how long it took. That teaches you what your specific stove's "medium-high" actually
means. Then cook below that setting permanently.

A cheap infrared thermometer removes the guesswork entirely — point at the empty pan, read
the number. Probably the highest-value purchase for anyone learning stainless.

## Hot handles

Hestan does not sell a handle cover, and third-party silicone sleeves are shaped for round
cast-iron handles, so they seat badly on the flat French-style handle and can slip under load.

Use a folded dry towel or a silicone pinch grip instead. After an oven finish, the handle
looks identical to a cold one — drape a towel over it the moment the pan comes out.
